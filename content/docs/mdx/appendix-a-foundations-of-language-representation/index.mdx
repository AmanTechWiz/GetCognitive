# Appendix A — Foundations of Language Representation

This appendix answers one question: **how does text become something a computer can think with?**

At the lowest level, every language model is doing arithmetic on numbers organized in grids. But language isn't numbers. "Cat" isn't a number. "The quick brown fox" isn't a matrix. So before any of the sophisticated stuff — attention, transformers, training — someone had to figure out how to bridge that gap. This appendix is about that bridge.

You don't need any prior experience with machine learning. If you know what a list of numbers is, you have enough background to follow everything here.

---

## What's in this appendix

**Chapter 1 — Why Computers Can't Understand Language**

The fundamental problem: computers work natively with numbers, but language is symbolic. We explore why the naive approach (ASCII codes) completely fails, what a *good* numerical representation would need to look like, and why this question is harder than it first appears.

**Chapter 2 — Tokens and Tokenization**

Before a model sees your text, it gets chopped into pieces called *tokens*. This chapter covers how and why — starting with the simplest possible approaches, showing exactly why each falls short, and building up to BPE, the algorithm used by essentially every modern LLM. Includes an interactive notebook where you build all three tokenizers from scratch in Python.

**Chapter 3 — Embeddings: Giving Tokens Meaning**

Token IDs are still just arbitrary integers. Embeddings are the step that turns those integers into vectors with genuine semantic content — where "cat" and "kitten" end up mathematically close, and word arithmetic like `king − man + woman ≈ queen` actually works. Diagrams and notebook included.

**Chapter 4 — Tensors: The Data Structure Behind Everything**

Every number in a neural network lives in a tensor. This chapter explains what tensors are, how to read their shapes, and how the data you've seen so far (token IDs, embedding vectors, batches of sentences) maps to specific tensor shapes. Once you can read tensor shapes, you can read any ML code.

**Chapter 5 — Vector Databases: Putting Embeddings to Work**

You have embeddings — now what do you do with a million of them? This chapter covers semantic search, how vector databases index and retrieve vectors at scale using ANN algorithms, and how RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) plugs retrieved context into a language model. The applied payoff from Chapters 3 and 4.

**Chapter 6 — The Math Bridge**

Three operations that everything in neural networks is built on: the **dot product** (measures similarity, the fundamental unit of neural computation), **matrix multiplication** (many dot products at once — one matrix multiply = one neural network layer), and **activation functions** (ReLU, GELU, softmax — why they're needed and what they do). Ends exactly where Appendix B picks up.

## A note on structure

Each chapter is self-contained. You don't need to read them in order — if you're here to understand one specific thing, jump directly to it. They're also independent from the main handbook; you can read them any time.

That said, the natural reading order is 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 5 → 6, because each chapter uses vocabulary introduced in the previous one. And Chapter 6 is specifically designed as the bridge into Appendix B.
