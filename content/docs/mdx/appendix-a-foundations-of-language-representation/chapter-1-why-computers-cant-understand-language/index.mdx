# 1. Why Computers Can't Understand Language

## The Problem: Language is Not Numbers

Computers are fundamentally numerical machines. Everything they do—from arithmetic to graphics rendering—ultimately comes down to manipulating numbers. Yet human language is fundamentally symbolic. The word "cat" is not a number; it's a sequence of letters that humans agree represents a furry feline animal.

This creates a profound gap: **how do we bridge the symbolic nature of language with the numerical nature of computation?**

Consider what happens when you ask a computer to determine if two words are similar:
- A human instantly knows "cat" and "kitten" are related
- A computer only sees different sequences of bytes
- A computer cannot inherently understand that both refer to similar concepts

## Why Raw Text Won't Work

You might think we could simply convert each letter to its ASCII code and pass that to a model. The letter 'c' becomes 99, 'a' becomes 97, and 't' becomes 116. But this approach has critical problems:

1. **No semantic meaning**: The sequence [99, 97, 116] contains no information about what a cat is
2. **Variable length**: Different words have different numbers of characters, making uniform processing difficult
3. **No relationships**: There's nothing in these numbers that tells us 'c', 'a', and 't' form a meaningful unit together
4. **No generalization**: The computer has no way to know that 'cat' and 'dog' are both animals

## The Solution: Numerical Representations

Instead of trying to process raw text, we need to represent **meaning** numerically. This means:
- Converting text into fixed-size numerical vectors
- Ensuring these vectors encode semantic and syntactic information
- Creating a space where similar concepts have similar numerical representations

This is the core insight that makes modern language models possible: **if we can represent language as numbers in a clever way, we can use mathematical operations to understand and generate language.**
